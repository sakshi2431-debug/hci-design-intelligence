# People-centered product design

Use this reference when shaping a product or feature, defining value, planning growth, improving engagement or retention, choosing metrics, designing monetization, prioritizing a roadmap, or evaluating whether an experience serves people over time.

## North star

A good product helps people make meaningful progress under real constraints while sustaining a viable value exchange with the organization. People-centered design is not anti-business, anti-persuasion, or anti-engagement. It asks whether product success is caused by genuine value rather than confusion, compulsion, exclusion, artificial friction, or the transfer of hidden costs to users and society.

Wellbeing is contextual rather than a single universal score. Consider at least:

- **Agency:** can people understand, choose, refuse, change course, and leave?
- **Capability:** does the product help them accomplish or learn something they value?
- **Access:** who can participate, under what physical, cognitive, linguistic, financial, and technical conditions?
- **Experience:** what attention, effort, emotion, uncertainty, and social exposure does use require?
- **Consequences:** what persists after the interaction for the person, other people, the organization, and the wider system?

Use [hci-foundations.md](hci-foundations.md) for interaction mechanisms, [open-access-theory-ledger.md](open-access-theory-ledger.md) for Self-Determination Theory, COM-B, Value Sensitive Design, Design Justice, and related theory, and [ethics-privacy-deceptive-design.md](ethics-privacy-deceptive-design.md) for rights and manipulation risks.

## Frame the opportunity

Before proposing features, make a compact outcome model:

1. **People:** whose goals matter, including non-users and people indirectly affected?
2. **Progress:** what are they trying to achieve, preserve, understand, feel, avoid, or become?
3. **Context:** what constraints, routines, relationships, abilities, risks, and alternatives shape the behavior?
4. **Product promise:** what value does the product claim to create and what must it do reliably?
5. **Organization:** what durable outcome makes providing that value viable?
6. **Behavior:** what voluntary behavior connects the experience to both outcomes?
7. **Tradeoffs:** where can the outcomes diverge, and who bears the cost?
8. **Evidence:** what is known, assumed, inferred, and still untested?

Do not collapse a heterogeneous population into an average “user.” Identify who benefits, who struggles, who is excluded, and who may be overexposed to the product's risks.

## Model the value exchange

Express the intended relationship explicitly:

`person's meaningful progress -> experienced value -> voluntary behavior -> legitimate product outcome -> capacity to sustain or improve the value`

This is a hypothesis, not a fact. Look for broken links:

- activity increases without meaningful progress;
- conversion relies on misunderstanding or urgency;
- retention is caused by lock-in, accumulated loss, or difficult exit;
- efficiency for the organization transfers labor or risk to the person;
- personalization improves response while reducing autonomy, privacy, diversity, or discovery;
- benefits accrue to the direct user while harms accrue to workers, communities, or non-users.

## Distinguish engagement from value

Engagement is behavior, not automatically a benefit. Classify the intended engagement:

- **Instrumental:** completing a task, learning, coordinating, creating, deciding, or receiving support.
- **Experiential:** enjoyment, exploration, expression, connection, or restoration is itself part of the value.
- **Contributory:** creating value for a community or shared system.
- **Extractive:** behavior primarily produces attention, data, spend, dependence, or exposure without proportionate user benefit.

The same behavior can move between categories depending on context, frequency, age, vulnerability, and the product's business model. Do not infer healthy engagement from frequency alone.

Design for an appropriate rhythm:

- fast completion when the goal is completion;
- deep involvement when immersion serves the chosen experience;
- return when new value exists, not merely when a notification can provoke it;
- pause, stopping, switching, export, cancellation, and deletion when those serve the person;
- re-entry with context and recovery rather than punishment.

## Build a balanced measurement system

Never optimize one proxy in isolation. Use four linked layers:

1. **Human outcome:** evidence of meaningful progress, capability, comprehension, quality, satisfaction with consequences, or another context-specific benefit.
2. **Behavior/product outcome:** adoption, successful use, retention, contribution, conversion, revenue, efficiency, or reliability.
3. **Experience quality:** success, effort, errors, accessibility, recovery, perceived control, trust calibration, and expectation fit.
4. **Guardrails:** regret, complaints, unwanted use, failed cancellation, overuse, exclusion, privacy incidents, unequal outcomes, downstream support burden, or safety events.

For every metric, state:

- construct and operational definition;
- whose outcome it represents;
- desired direction and meaningful magnitude;
- segment and time horizon;
- how it can be gamed or misread;
- countermetric or guardrail;
- decision the result will change.

Measure distributions and important segments, not only averages. A positive aggregate can conceal severe harm or exclusion for a smaller group.

## Evaluate growth proposals

For a proposed growth, conversion, retention, monetization, or engagement intervention, examine:

1. **Value mechanism:** why should this behavior help the person?
2. **Business mechanism:** how does it create legitimate organizational value?
3. **Autonomy:** is the choice informed, reversible, and free from coercive asymmetry?
4. **Attention and emotion:** does the design support the goal or exploit urgency, anxiety, status, loss, or variable reward?
5. **Distribution:** which groups gain, lose, disengage, or become vulnerable?
6. **Time:** what happens after repeated exposure, habit formation, changing circumstances, or attempted exit?
7. **Alternatives:** can the same outcome be achieved with less burden or risk?
8. **Evidence:** what test can distinguish real value from metric movement?

Persuasive design is acceptable when it transparently helps people act on goals they endorse. It becomes suspect when the product hides consequences, manufactures pressure, obstructs alternatives, or benefits from behavior people would reject under clearer conditions.

## Experiment without losing the person

Define the causal hypothesis connecting intervention, experience, behavior, human outcome, and business result. Measure the intended benefit and plausible harms. Predefine segments, guardrails, stopping rules, minimum meaningful effects, and the decision threshold. Include qualitative or longitudinal follow-up when short-term telemetry cannot reveal comprehension, regret, habit, or downstream consequences.

Do not call a test successful because its target metric increased. Interpret it as successful only when the value mechanism remains plausible, guardrails are acceptable, important groups are not harmed, and the effect supports a durable product decision. Apply [research-rigor.md](research-rigor.md) for consequential claims.

## Make decisions explicit

For meaningful product recommendations, report:

- intended human and product outcomes;
- target population and affected non-users;
- value-exchange hypothesis;
- evidence and assumptions;
- proposed experience or intervention;
- expected benefits and costs;
- accessibility, agency, privacy, safety, and wellbeing implications;
- success metrics, guardrails, segments, and time horizon;
- cheapest useful validation and reversal plan.

Do not moralize ordinary tradeoffs or claim that every product must maximize every dimension of wellbeing. Make the tradeoff legible, protect non-negotiable rights and safety, and help the team choose with evidence.
