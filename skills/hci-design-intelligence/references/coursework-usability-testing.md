# Coursework synthesis: Usability Testing

Public-safe original synthesis derived from user-supplied graduate coursework. It does not reproduce readings, assignments, rubrics, private examples, or participant data. Treat it as a course-informed lens, not as independent empirical authority.

## Use this reference when

Planning, conducting, analyzing, or reporting usability tests and expert inspections.

## Choose the evidence source deliberately

- **Usability testing:** representative people attempt representative tasks; useful for observing interaction and breakdowns.
- **Heuristic evaluation:** evaluators inspect against defined heuristics; useful for generating potential issues, not estimating user prevalence.
- **Cognitive walkthrough:** evaluators step through learnability for specified users, goals, actions, cues, and feedback.
- **Field research:** reveals situated work, constraints, adaptations, and service context.
- **Unmoderated remote testing:** supports distributed participation and repeated tasks but reduces observation and facilitation control.

## Study design

Define the decision, research questions, participant criteria, task scenarios, prototype fidelity, environment, facilitation, measures, data handling, analysis plan, and stopping logic. Pilot the session before recruitment is complete.

Write tasks around goals and context without revealing the intended path. Capture success, path, errors, assistance, time where meaningful, behavior, comments, and system state. Avoid turning think-aloud comments into direct truth about cognition.

## Inspection and severity

For each potential issue, record the violated principle or breakdown, context, evidence, consequence, affected task or people, frequency evidence if available, persistence, recoverability, confidence, and recommendation. Severity is a decision aid—not a property that can be assigned reliably without context.

## Synthesis and prioritization

- Preserve observations before collapsing them into themes.
- Separate participant behavior, participant interpretation, evaluator inference, and recommendation.
- Look for patterns and counterexamples across users, tasks, states, and contexts.
- Prioritize using harm, rights, task criticality, reach, frequency evidence, persistence, recoverability, dependencies, and effort.
- Quantitative prioritization frameworks can organize discussion but should not create false precision.

## Reporting

Make the report useful to decision-makers: decision context, method, participants, limitations, strengths to preserve, prioritized findings, evidence, recommendations, owners, and verification. Do not generalize a small qualitative sample into population prevalence.
