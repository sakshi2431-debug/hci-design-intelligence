# Product critique and design judgment

Use this reference to critique a whole product, facilitate a design review, or develop a defensible quality bar. A critique is a decision-making practice, not a performance of taste and not a substitute for research.

## Frame the critique

Establish before judging:

- the people, context, job or progress, and affected non-users;
- the product promise, intended human outcome, and legitimate business outcome;
- the artifact's maturity and the decision the critique must inform;
- known evidence, constraints, deliberate tradeoffs, and unresolved assumptions;
- the comparison set: prior version, direct alternative, non-consumption, or a simpler workflow.

Critique the fidelity that exists. Do not demand production polish from a concept sketch or use a polished prototype as evidence that the underlying need is real.

## Inspect the product at five levels

1. **Value:** Is the product solving a meaningful problem for the intended people? Is the value exchange fair and legible?
2. **Experience:** Does the end-to-end journey form a coherent conceptual model across discovery, onboarding, use, interruption, recovery, support, cancellation, and exit?
3. **Interaction:** Are actions discoverable, understandable, controllable, accessible, and recoverable? Do feedback and system status support confident action?
4. **System:** Do content, data, AI behavior, service operations, policies, channels, and technical constraints support the promised experience?
5. **Craft:** Do hierarchy, language, typography, spacing, motion, and visual character reinforce meaning and brand without obscuring the task?

Include feasibility, viability, accessibility, safety, privacy, and distributional effects where relevant. A visual-only critique is incomplete for a product decision.

## Separate judgment from evidence

For each observation distinguish:

- **Observation:** what is present, absent, or observed in use;
- **Interpretation:** what it may communicate or cause;
- **Evidence:** research, analytics, support signals, standards, or project constraints;
- **Taste judgment:** a quality preference or comparative judgment that may still be valuable but is not user evidence;
- **Recommendation:** the change, preserved tradeoff, and expected outcome;
- **Validation:** what would show that the recommendation helped.

Good taste compresses experience, comparison, and sensitivity to coherence. It can identify promising questions and quality gaps; it cannot establish population needs or causal effects by authority. When disagreement is aesthetic, name it. When it affects comprehension, access, trust, task success, or product value, connect it to evidence and a test.

## Facilitate useful critique

- Invite the smallest useful mix of product, design, engineering, research, content, accessibility, operations, support, and domain expertise.
- State the decision and feedback scope. Ask the presenter to name intent and constraints before solutions are proposed.
- Let participants observe independently before discussion to reduce anchoring and hierarchy effects.
- Start with what the design is trying to accomplish and what should be preserved.
- Use questions and specific consequences instead of verdicts about the designer.
- Keep divergent observations separate from convergent prioritization.
- End with owners, evidence needs, decisions, and what will not change.

Avoid critique theater: vague reactions, status-driven opinions, competitive mimicry, solution dumping, consensus as proof, and a long issue list without priorities.

## High-leverage questions

- What progress is this product helping whom make, under what conditions?
- What is the central promise, and where does the experience contradict it?
- Which behavior feels coherent and intentional; which feels accidental or inherited?
- What tradeoff is the design making, and who receives the benefit or cost?
- What are we observing, what are we inferring, and what do we actually know?
- What single change would most improve the end-to-end experience without damaging a strength?
- What evidence would distinguish a taste preference from a user or product risk?

Produce a short product thesis, preserved strengths, prioritized findings, unresolved disagreements, decisions, and validation plan. ## Provenance

This is an original synthesis grounded in HCI evidence discipline and informed by Atlassian's practitioner overview, [How to lead a product critique](https://www.atlassian.com/agile/product-management/product-critique). Atlassian describes critique as a structured, cross-functional practice spanning value, usability, design, functionality, accessibility, feasibility, and business considerations throughout the lifecycle. Treat that page as practitioner guidance, not research evidence. Do not treat generated persona feedback as user research.
